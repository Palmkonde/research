# scikit-learn User Guide, "3.4. Metrics and scoring: quantifying the quality of predictions" (1.9.1)

Supports: `confusion_matrix` rows are the true class, with a warning that other references use a different axis convention; the binary terms tp, fp, fn, tn; the precision, recall and $F_\beta$ formulas, including the $(1+\beta^2)\,tp$ form used to avoid division by zero; recall is also called sensitivity and true positive rate, specificity is also called true negative rate; accuracy as the fraction of correct predictions; balanced accuracy as the mean of sensitivity and specificity, and its warning about accuracy inflated by an imbalanced test set. Also (for the ROC/PR Finding): the ROC curve plots TPR against FPR as the threshold varies, and `roc_auc_score` summarizes it as one number (Section 3.4.4.15); `precision_recall_curve` varies a decision threshold, AP is $\sum_n (R_n - R_{n-1}) P_n$, random predictions give an AP equal to the fraction of positives, interpolated AP variants (Manning 2008, Everingham 2010) are not implemented, and Davis 2006 and Flach 2015 explain why linear interpolation of PR points, as used by the trapezoidal `auc`, is overly optimistic, with Chen 2024 benchmarking interpolation strategies (Section 3.4.4.9) Also (for the class-imbalance Finding): balanced accuracy "avoids inflated performance estimates on imbalanced datasets", is equal to accuracy on balanced data, equals the ROC AUC of binary predictions in the binary case, and with `adjusted=True` is Youden's J statistic (informedness), with random scoring 0 (Section 3.4.4.4); Cohen's kappa "is intended to compare labelings by different human annotators, not a classifier versus a ground truth" (Section 3.4.4.5); the binary MCC $= (tp \cdot tn - fp \cdot fn)/\sqrt{(tp+fp)(tp+fn)(tn+fp)(tn+fn)}$, in essence a correlation coefficient with +1 perfect, 0 average random and −1 inverse prediction (Section 3.4.4.13). Also (for the multiclass-averaging Finding): binary metrics are extended to multiclass by treating the data as a collection of binary problems, one per class, and averaging; macro gives equal weight to each class, may highlight infrequent but important classes, and may over-emphasize the typically low performance on an infrequent class; weighted weights each class's score by its presence in the true data; micro gives each sample-class pair an equal contribution by summing dividends and divisors, and may be preferred in multilabel settings and in multiclass classification where a majority class is to be ignored; samples applies only to multilabel problems; `average=None` returns the per-class scores (Section 3.4.4.1); balanced accuracy is the macro-average of per-class recall (Section 3.4.4.4); precision, recall and F apply to each label independently, micro-averaging with all labels included gives precision, recall and F identical to accuracy, weighted averaging may give an F-score not between precision and recall, and macro F is the arithmetic mean over per-class F, not the harmonic mean of the arithmetic precision and recall means, citing Opitz and Burst 2019; the set-notation table of micro, samples, macro and weighted P, R and $F_\beta$; the `labels=[1, 2]` example that excludes a negative class from micro recall (Section 3.4.4.9.2); multiclass `roc_auc_score` supports one-vs-one and one-vs-rest, Hand and Till's OvO formula $\frac{1}{c(c-1)}\sum_{j}\sum_{k>j}(\text{AUC}(j \mid k) + \text{AUC}(k \mid j))$ with $\text{AUC}(j \mid k) \ne \text{AUC}(k \mid j)$ in general, its prevalence-weighted version with $p(j \cup k)$, and OvR with macro, weighted and micro averaging (Section 3.4.4.15.2). Also (for the reporting Finding): dummy estimators give "a baseline value of those metrics for random predictions" (Section 3.4.2); comparing an estimator against simple rules of thumb is a sanity check; `DummyClassifier` strategies are `stratified` (random predictions respecting the training class distribution), `most_frequent` (always the most frequent training label), `prior` (like `most_frequent`, with `predict_proba` returning the class prior), `uniform` (uniformly random) and `constant` (a user-given label, motivated by F1 scoring when the positive class is in the minority), and all of them ignore the input data; in the guide's example a linear-kernel SVC scores 0.63 against a `most_frequent` dummy's 0.579, and an RBF-kernel SVC 0.94; accuracy "too close to random" probably means something went wrong, such as unhelpful features, a badly tuned hyperparameter or class imbalance (Section 3.4.8).
